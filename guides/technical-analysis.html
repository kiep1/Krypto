Technical analysis for beginners: a chart-reading checklist

Technical analysis is a way to describe and compare information on a price chart. This checklist focuses on careful observation rather than prediction.

AI-assisted educational content · Reviewed for consistency · Not reviewed by an investment adviser · Updated October 24, 2026

Start with the chart, not the conclusion

Before naming a trend or pattern, check what the image actually shows. Look for a readable instrument label, timeframe, price scale, and enough surrounding price action to understand the context. If any of those details are missing, note the limitation.

Then describe visible facts in plain language. For example: “price has made two higher swing highs in the visible section” is an observation. “Price must keep rising” is a prediction and is not established by that observation.

Describe structure on the visible timeframe

Chart structure is a shorthand for the arrangement of visible highs, lows, and ranges. A sequence of higher highs and higher lows may be described as upward structure on that section of the chart. Lower highs and lower lows may be described as downward structure. Repeated movement between similar areas may look range-bound.

These labels depend on the selected timeframe and the portion of the chart shown. A short-term pattern can sit inside a different longer-term structure. Avoid describing one screenshot as the complete state of a market.

Mark areas, not certainties

Support and resistance are commonly used names for areas where price has previously paused, reversed, or spent time. Real charts rarely respect a perfectly exact line. Treat a level as a zone and ask what evidence supports it.

Not every visible line is important. A line becomes a useful study point only when it is clearly tied to visible price behavior and the screenshot provides enough context to read it. See the guide to support and resistance zones.

Compare scenarios instead of announcing an outcome

A scenario is a conditional explanation: if a visible condition holds, one interpretation may remain plausible; if it fails, the interpretation may weaken. A balanced chart note also records what is unknown and what would change the view.

For example, an apparent breakout can be described as a move beyond a visible range. The image alone may not show whether that move will continue, reverse, or be affected by news or liquidity. A chart pattern is a hypothesis, as explained in how to read patterns carefully.

A repeatable checklist

  1. What instrument, timeframe, and price range are actually visible?
  2. What are the most obvious highs, lows, and areas of congestion?
  3. Which parts are observations, and which parts are interpretations?
  4. What alternative explanation could fit the same image?
  5. What visible change would weaken the interpretation?
  6. What important information is missing from the screenshot?

This checklist is for education, not a method for selecting trades. An interpretation can be internally consistent and still fail. Read risk management basics for a discussion of uncertainty and why chart analysis cannot decide personal risk for you.
