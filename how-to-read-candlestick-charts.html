CHART READING FOUNDATIONS

How to read candlestick charts: a beginner’s guide

A candlestick chart packs four prices into every bar. This guide explains what each part means, how timeframes change the picture, and how to describe a chart without jumping to predictions.

AI-assisted educational content · Reviewed for consistency · Not reviewed by an investment adviser · Updated November 7, 2026

What a candlestick shows

A candlestick summarises everything that happened to a price during one period. Each period gets one candle, and each candle records four prices: the open (the first trade of the period), the high (the highest trade), the low (the lowest trade) and the close (the last trade of the period).

The thick part of the candle is the body. It spans from the open to the close. The thin lines above and below are the wicks (also called shadows or tails) and they reach out to the high and the low. Candlestick charts became popular with Western traders largely through Steve Nison’s 1991 book on Japanese candlestick techniques, and most charting platforms now use them by default.

Colour is only a convention. Many platforms draw a candle green (or white) when the close is above the open and red (or black) when the close is below the open, but you can change this, and some platforms reverse it. Always check the legend before assuming what a colour means.

Reading the body and the wicks

Once you know the four prices, a single candle tells a small story about that period. These are descriptions of what happened, not forecasts:

  • A long body means the close was far from the open. Price moved a lot in one direction during the period.
  • A short body means the open and close were close together. Price may have moved around but ended near where it began.
  • A long upper wick means price traded well above the close but did not hold those levels by the end of the period.
  • A long lower wick means price traded well below the close but recovered before the period ended.
  • Almost no wicks means the open and close were close to the high and low, so price travelled mostly in one direction.

Notice that each statement is about the past. “Price was rejected from a level” is an observation. “Price will now fall” is a prediction that the candle alone cannot support. Keeping those two apart is the single most useful habit a beginner can build.

Timeframes: what one candle represents

The timeframe sets how much time each candle covers. On a 1-hour chart every candle is one hour of trading; on a daily chart every candle is one day. The same market looks very different depending on the choice:

  • Lower timeframes (1 to 15 minutes) show more candles and more noise. Small moves look large and patterns appear and disappear quickly.
  • Middle timeframes (1 to 4 hours) are a common compromise for people who check charts a few times a day.
  • Higher timeframes (daily, weekly) show fewer candles and filter out much of the noise, which is why many people check them first to understand the bigger context.

A useful routine is to start on a higher timeframe, note the broad structure, then zoom in. A move that looks like a collapse on a 5-minute chart can be a small pullback on a daily chart. See technical analysis for beginners for a step-by-step checklist.

Crypto markets trade around the clock, so candles open and close at times set by the exchange or charting platform (often midnight UTC for daily candles). Two platforms can draw slightly different daily candles for the same asset because their reference times or data sources differ.

Reading a sequence of candles

One candle is a fragment. The useful information comes from how candles relate to each other:

  • Higher highs and higher lows across several swings can be described as upward structure.
  • Lower highs and lower lows can be described as downward structure.
  • Repeated reversals between similar prices look like a range.
  • Large candles after quiet candles show a change in activity, though not its cause.

Look for where candles close, not just where wicks reach. A close is the price the market settled on for the period, so many chart readers give it more weight than an intraperiod spike. Areas where price repeatedly stalled or reversed are covered in support and resistance zones.

Volume and chart scale

Most platforms show volume as bars under the price chart. Volume is the amount traded during the period. A large move on unusually high volume involved more participation than the same move on low volume, although volume figures can differ between exchanges and are not a guarantee of anything.

Also check the price scale. A linear scale gives equal distance to equal price changes; a logarithmic scale gives equal distance to equal percentage changes. On long histories of fast-growing assets, such as many cryptocurrencies, the two scales can make the same history look very different. If a screenshot does not show the scale, note that limitation before drawing conclusions.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Reading one candle as a signal. A single candle rarely carries enough context. See candlestick patterns explained for how patterns depend on location and confirmation.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. A pattern on a 1-minute chart and the same pattern on a weekly chart are not equivalent.
  • Hindsight bias. On a finished chart the “obvious” turning point is easy to see. At the right edge of the chart, with candles still forming, it is not.
  • Confusing a story with evidence. “Sellers are exhausted” sounds convincing but is not visible on the chart. Write down what you can see separately from what you think it means.
  • Forgetting risk. Even a well-read chart can be wrong, so decisions about size and exits belong in a separate process, covered in risk management basics.

A simple practice routine

  1. Open a chart on a daily timeframe and scroll back a few months.
  2. Pick any ten candles and write down their open, high, low and close from the chart’s data window.
  3. Describe each candle in one sentence using only observations (“long upper wick, closed near the low”).
  4. Mark the most obvious swing highs and swing lows and describe the structure.
  5. Scroll forward one candle at a time and note what you got right, wrong or could not have known.

Repeating this on different markets and timeframes trains you to describe charts carefully before forming opinions. When you are ready, you can upload a screenshot to the analyzer and compare its description with yours.

Frequently asked questions

What do the colours of candlesticks mean?

By convention, one colour (often green) marks a candle that closed above its open and another (often red) marks one that closed below its open. The choice is a platform setting, so check the legend.

What is the best timeframe for beginners?

There is no universal best. Higher timeframes such as daily show fewer candles and less noise, which many beginners find easier to study. The right choice depends on how often you look at charts and how long you plan to hold.

Can candlesticks predict price?

Candlesticks describe what has already happened. They can help you organise evidence and compare scenarios, but they do not tell you what will happen next.

Are candlestick charts better than line charts?

They show more information per period (open, high, low and close rather than only the close), which is why many people prefer them. Line charts are cleaner for viewing long-term trends.
