PATTERN READING

Candlestick patterns explained: doji, hammer, engulfing and more

Candlestick patterns are named shapes made by one to three candles. This guide describes the most common ones, what each shows, and why context matters more than the name.

AI-assisted educational content · Reviewed for consistency · Not reviewed by an investment adviser · Updated November 7, 2026

What candlestick patterns are

A candlestick pattern is a recurring arrangement of one or more candles that traders have given a name. Each name is shorthand for a description of price behaviour, such as “price fell, then recovered most of the move within the same period”. If you are new to candles, start with how to read candlestick charts.

It helps to treat every pattern as a hypothesis rather than a signal. The pattern says something about how one period ended; whether it matters depends on where it appears and what happens afterwards.

Common single-candle patterns

Doji

A doji has an open and close at, or almost at, the same price, so the body is tiny or flat. It shows indecision or balance during that period: price moved but ended where it began. A doji after a long move may deserve a closer look, but on its own it does not say which way price will go next.

Spinning top

A spinning top has a small body with wicks on both sides. Like the doji it shows a period where neither buyers nor sellers ended in control, though the open and close were not identical.

Marubozu

A marubozu has a long body and little or no wicks. Price opened near one extreme and closed near the other, so it moved in one direction almost all period. It is a strong description of that period, not a forecast of the next.

Hammer and hanging man

Both have a small body near the top of the candle and a long lower wick, often at least twice the body’s length, with little or no upper wick. The shape is identical; the name depends on context. After a decline it is usually called a hammer, because price fell sharply but recovered. After an advance it is called a hanging man, because the long lower wick shows sellers were able to push price down hard even though it recovered.

Shooting star and inverted hammer

These are the mirror images: a small body near the bottom and a long upper wick. After an advance it is called a shooting star (price rose but could not hold the highs). After a decline the same shape is called an inverted hammer.

Common multi-candle patterns

Bullish and bearish engulfing

An engulfing pattern is two candles. In a bullish engulfing pattern a down candle is followed by an up candle whose body completely covers the previous body. A bearish engulfing is the reverse. The larger second body shows that the move of the previous period was fully reversed within one period.

Harami

A harami is a large candle followed by a smaller candle whose body sits inside the first candle’s body. It shows that momentum slowed, which is the opposite of the engulfing idea.

Morning star and evening star

These are three-candle patterns. A morning star is a large down candle, a small-bodied candle (often a doji or spinning top), and then a large up candle that closes well into the first candle’s body. An evening star is the mirror image after an advance. They describe a sequence in which a move stalled and then reversed.

Piercing line and dark cloud cover

In a piercing line a down candle is followed by an up candle that closes above the midpoint of the first body but not above its open. A dark cloud cover is the opposite after an advance. These are weaker versions of the engulfing idea.

Why context matters more than the name

The same shape can mean very different things depending on its surroundings. Before taking a pattern seriously, check:

  • Location. Did it form at a level where price has previously reacted? A hammer in the middle of nowhere carries less information than one at a clearly tested area. See support and resistance zones.
  • The move before it. Reversal patterns assume there was something to reverse. A “hammer” after a tiny drift is not the same as one after a sharp fall.
  • Timeframe. Patterns on higher timeframes summarise more trading and are generally less noisy than those on a 1-minute chart.
  • Confirmation. What did the following candles do? A pattern that is not followed by the expected behaviour has failed, and recognising failure is part of the process.
  • Volume. Unusually active periods can say more about participation than quiet ones, though volume data varies by venue.

How reliable are candlestick patterns?

Honestly, less than most social-media content suggests. Academic and practitioner studies of candlestick patterns have produced mixed results: some find small effects in certain markets and periods, while others find that the effects disappear after trading costs or fail to hold on new data. Many patterns are also defined loosely, so two people can disagree about whether a candle “counts”.

For that reason it is safer to use patterns to organise observations and form scenarios than to treat them as buy or sell triggers. A scenario states what you see, what would support it, and what would invalidate it. That framing is how CandleTutor’s chart analysis works as well, and it is also why risk planning should come first, as discussed in risk management basics. For multi-candle shapes that develop over weeks rather than days, see how to read chart patterns carefully.

A checklist before you trust a pattern

  1. Which timeframe is the pattern on, and what does the higher timeframe look like?
  2. Is there a clear prior move for the pattern to reverse or continue?
  3. Is it located at a visible area of previous reaction?
  4. What would confirm it, and what visible change would show it failed?
  5. What is the potential loss if it fails, and is that acceptable? (See the position size calculator.)

Frequently asked questions

What is the most reliable candlestick pattern?

No pattern is reliably predictive on its own. Patterns that form at meaningful areas on higher timeframes and are followed by confirming price action are generally treated with more weight, but none removes uncertainty.

Do candlestick patterns work in crypto?

The shapes can appear in any market. Crypto trades around the clock and can be very volatile, so single-candle signals can be noisy; context and risk control matter even more.

What is the difference between a hammer and a hanging man?

Only the context. Both have a small body and a long lower wick; a hammer appears after a decline, a hanging man after an advance.

How many candlestick patterns are there?

Dozens are named, and many are variations of a few ideas: indecision (doji), rejection (long wicks), reversal of the prior move (engulfing) and slowing momentum (harami). Learning those ideas is more useful than memorising names.
