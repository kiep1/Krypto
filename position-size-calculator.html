The formula

Position sizing starts from how much you are willing to lose if the trade fails, not from how much you want to buy:

  1. Amount at risk = account size × risk percentage.
  2. Risk per unit = the distance between your entry and your stop-loss.
  3. Position size = amount at risk ÷ risk per unit.

Worked example: with a 5,000 account and 1% risk, the amount at risk is 50. If the entry is 100 and the stop is 95, risk per unit is 5, so the position size is 50 ÷ 5 = 10 units, a position value of 1,000. If the stop is hit, the planned loss is about 50, or 1% of the account. A wider stop produces a smaller position and a tighter stop a larger one, but the planned loss stays the same.

Why small risk per trade matters

Losses compound against you, and recovery gets harder as the drawdown grows. A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to break even. Look at what ten losses in a row would do to an account at different risk levels:

Risk per tradeAccount after 10 straight lossesDrawdown
1%90.4%−9.6%
2%81.7%−18.3%
5%59.9%−40.1%
10%34.9%−65.1%

Even a method with a good hit rate will have losing streaks. Many educators suggest keeping the risk per trade to a small single-digit percentage, often 1% or less for beginners, but the right figure depends on your situation and tolerance and is a personal decision.

Where the stop should come from

The stop-loss should be placed where your trade idea is shown to be wrong, for example beyond a level that, if broken, would invalidate your scenario (see support and resistance zones). The position size then adapts to that distance. The reverse approach, picking a position size first and then squeezing the stop to fit, tends to place stops at random points where ordinary noise can trigger them.

What the calculator does not include

  • Fees and spreads. They add to the real cost of every trade and enlarge losses.
  • Slippage and gaps. A stop order is not a guarantee of exit at that price; fast markets can fill you beyond it.
  • Leverage. Borrowed exposure can lose the whole margin quickly, and liquidation can happen before your stop in extreme conditions.
  • Correlation. Several positions in similar assets, such as many cryptocurrencies, can lose together, so the combined risk is larger than each position suggests.

For the wider picture, read risk management basics. To practise reading the chart that leads to the stop and entry, start with technical analysis for beginners.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of my account should I risk per trade?

There is no single right answer. Many educators suggest small amounts, often 1% or less for beginners, so that a losing streak does not cause serious damage. The decision depends on your finances and tolerance for loss.

Does leverage change the position size formula?

The risk-based formula stays the same: the planned loss is still the account size times the risk percentage. Leverage only changes how much margin you need, and it adds liquidation risk.

Can I use this for crypto, forex and stocks?

The arithmetic is the same for any market that quotes a price. Check the contract size or minimum order size of your platform, since the number of units may need to be rounded.
