UNCERTAINTY & LIMITS

Risk management basics for chart analysis

A chart can help frame a question. It cannot determine what level of financial risk is suitable for a particular person.

AI-assisted educational content · Reviewed for consistency · Not reviewed by an investment adviser · Updated November 7, 2026

Two different kinds of risk

When CandleTutor labels interpretation risk as low, moderate, high, or unknown, it is describing uncertainty in reading the screenshot. It is not measuring market volatility, calculating a portfolio loss, or assessing whether a decision is suitable for you.

Personal financial risk depends on circumstances the image cannot reveal, such as your resources, obligations, goals, experience, and the consequences of loss. The analyzer does not collect or evaluate that information.

Use invalidation as a reasoning tool

An invalidation condition explains what visible evidence would weaken a chart interpretation. It can make reasoning more precise: instead of saying “the pattern looks bullish,” describe what would have to remain true for that hypothesis to make sense and what would challenge it.

An invalidation level is not automatically a stop order or a recommendation. The analyzer may not be able to read a level reliably from a screenshot. It should say when it cannot determine one rather than inventing a price.

Account for what the image leaves out

A screenshot may omit the broader timeframe, order size, transaction costs, market depth, news, or later price movement. The AI report also does not fetch current market data. These gaps can change how someone interprets the same chart.

Before relying on any explanation, ask whether it depends on something outside the image. If it does, the screenshot cannot settle the question on its own.

Questions to ask before acting on any chart idea

  • Am I treating a possible scenario as a certainty?
  • What evidence is visible, and what am I assuming?
  • What could make this interpretation wrong?
  • Have I checked current, reliable information beyond the screenshot?
  • Am I making a personal financial decision that needs independent advice?

These questions support careful learning; they do not replace a personalized risk plan. Learn more about reading a chart systematically and treating patterns as hypotheses.

Decide the loss before the trade

The most practical risk habit is to set a maximum loss per trade as a percentage of your account before you enter, then size the position so that a stop at your invalidation level costs no more than that. The position size calculator does the arithmetic. Small, consistent risk keeps a losing streak survivable; the table in that guide shows how fast larger risk percentages compound into serious drawdowns.

Reward-to-risk and expectancy

Reward-to-risk compares the potential gain with the planned loss. A trade that risks 1 to make 2 has a 2:1 ratio. The ratio alone is not enough, because it also depends on how often trades win. Expectancy per trade is: (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss). For example, a 40% win rate with an average win of 2 and an average loss of 1 gives 0.4 × 2 − 0.6 × 1 = +0.2 per unit risked, before costs. With a 30% win rate the same ratio gives 0.3 × 2 − 0.7 × 1 = −0.1. Fees and slippage reduce both numbers, so thin edges disappear easily.

Win rates are estimates from past trades and can change, so treat any calculation as an approximation.

Drawdown and recovery

Losses are asymmetric: a 10% loss needs about an 11% gain to recover, a 25% loss needs 33%, and a 50% loss needs 100%. Avoiding large drawdowns matters more than chasing large gains, because deep holes are very hard to climb out of.

Risks beyond the chart

  • Leverage magnifies gains and losses and can trigger liquidation.
  • Correlation: several positions in similar assets can lose together.
  • Liquidity and gaps: exits may fill far from your stop in fast markets.
  • Platform and custody risk, especially relevant in crypto.
  • Behavioural risk: revenge trading, overtrading and moving stops are common ways accounts shrink. A written plan and a trading journal help you notice them.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I risk per trade?

There is no universal figure. Many educators suggest small amounts, often 1% or less for beginners, and the right choice depends on your finances and tolerance for loss.

Is a stop-loss guaranteed?

No. Fast markets and gaps can fill an order at a worse price than the stop level, so real losses can exceed the plan.

Can I eliminate risk?

No. You can only choose how much to risk, keep it small and survivable, and avoid risking money you cannot afford to lose.
